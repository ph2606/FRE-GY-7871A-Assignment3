\section*{1. Answer and investment decision}
Iran-related conflict news coincides with a large change in the oil market, but this sample does not identify a stable, precisely estimated causal war-risk sensitivity across financial assets. The distinction matters: a strong oil-price trend is visible in the data; the portion caused by an isolated war-risk factor is much harder to establish.

From 2 January to 15 September, WTI spot rises from \$57.21 to \$107.02 and Brent spot from \$61.98 to \$130.80. These are simple price increases of 87.07\% and 111.04\%. The \textbf{Brent minus WTI spot spread widens from \$4.77 to \$23.78 per barrel}. At the same time, equities finish above their January levels and Treasury yields rise. Those full-period changes include many other economic developments.

The study combines the covariance-difference method in Rigobon and Sack's \emph{Effects of War Risk} with an Iran-specific news corpus and the identification framework in Rigobon's \emph{Identification through Heteroskedasticity}. There are 24 source-dated event sessions and 24 matched comparison sessions. In the centered adaptation, 13 of 17 outcome estimates fail a conservative diagnostic for a positive, sufficiently clear change in the two-year-yield variance. The remaining estimates also have wide intervals or poor agreement between the identifying moments. The six-market covariance difference has a rank-one approximation residual of 0.501; this is a diagnostic, not a formal rejection test.

The separate NLP regressions measure associations with changes in news attention and negative vocabulary describing physical conflict and economic conditions. %%P_UNADJUSTED%% of %%PRIMARY_TESTS%% estimable coefficients have unadjusted $p<0.05$, and \textbf{none survives the multiple-test adjustment}. Strict text and interval requirements leave only %%REG_MIN_N%%--%%REG_MAX_N%% observations per model. Attention and the published global geopolitical-risk index are correlated at %%BENCH_ATT%% in their common %%BENCH_N%%-date sample. Negative vocabulary has little correlation with that index. These are different constructs.

\textbf{My decision is to allocate 10\% of an illustrative diversified portfolio to a Treasury bill with about three months remaining, while retaining the diversified core.} I would not initiate a leveraged directional oil or Brent--WTI spread position from these text measures. The decision uses short-duration carry and a limited risk budget because the estimated timing signal is weak. At the 15 September three-month constant-maturity yield of 4.11\%, approximate three-month carry is 1.03\% on the bill allocation, or 0.103 percentage points on the whole portfolio, before costs. This benchmark is not an executable security quote.

The supplementary social-media analysis covers 403 retrieved Truth Social text candidates. It gives a neutral qualitative reading of wording and subjects, with source and attribution checks. It does not provide numerical ratings of a public official or policy, and it does not claim a complete X/Twitter or media-post census.

\section*{3. Oil trends and the meaning of the spread}
\fig{figure1_oil}{.92}
\textbf{Spread definition:} $S_t=P_t^{Brent,spot}-P_t^{WTI,spot}$, in dollars per barrel. A positive value means Brent is more expensive. Its change is $\Delta S_t=\Delta P_t^{Brent}-\Delta P_t^{WTI}$, using matching observation intervals. The 2026 sample has 165 valid spread changes. The observed level ranges from $-\$2.45$ to \$25.94; it is not constrained to be positive.

WTI is a Cushing benchmark; Brent reflects a different physical crude market. Geography, quality, transport, refining demand, inventories and delivery timing can affect the spread. Disruption to seaborne Middle Eastern supply is an economically plausible channel, but the measured spread is not a probability of war or a pure war premium. \href{https://www.eia.gov/todayinenergy/detail.php?id=67424}{EIA's first-quarter review} also distinguishes physical prices and futures contract months.

On 15 September the spot spread is \$23.78, while the difference between the two Yahoo rolling-futures quotes is about \$2.92. These are different instruments and potentially different delivery months; the second number is not a matched-maturity spread trade. The study never combines Brent spot with WTI futures.
\takeaway{A wide physical spread makes energy exposure and contract choice important. It does not by itself justify shorting Brent against WTI. A spread position needs matched delivery months, basis-risk analysis, transaction costs and a separate entry rule.}

\section*{4. Global financial trends}
\fig{figure2_markets}{.94}
The S\&P 500 gains 10.11\% from its first 2026 observation through 16 September, EFA 9.94\% and EEM 17.46\%; these are simple endpoint changes. EFA and EEM are USD-traded funds, so they combine foreign equity exposure and exchange-rate effects. They are not synchronized local-country index closes. GLD finishes 1.64\% below its first observation after substantial variation during the year.

The two-year yield rises from 3.47\% to 4.67\%, and the ten-year from 4.19\% to 5.00\%. The 10s2s slope therefore narrows from 72 to 33 basis points: an endpoint \emph{bear flattening}, since both yields rise and the short end rises more. This describes the whole period; it is not an estimated war shock.

Oil disruption can increase inflation pressure and push yields upward, while concern about activity or risk appetite can push yields downward. The 2003 study's reported Treasury-yield decline cannot be imposed on 2026. Likewise, DXY and the broad trade-weighted dollar can move differently because their baskets differ. Credit spreads measure compensation above a Treasury curve, not the same object as the Brent--WTI spread.
\takeaway{The observed rise in both Treasury yields makes a long-duration position an unreliable automatic hedge against this conflict. I prefer a short bill for the defensive allocation and retain geographic diversification rather than infer a general sell-equities rule from war headlines.}

\section*{5. What the NLP measures}
Article relevance requires conflict/energy language and an Iran-related entity in the headline, or at least two body sentences containing both contexts. The literal rules and all retained source metadata are available in the code and local audit tables. They retrieve a subject; they are not a trained accuracy-validated classifier.

\textbf{Attention} is the number of relevant articles assigned to a market session, divided by the number of calendar days since the preceding equity session. Thus a weekend interval is not treated as one calendar day. We use $A_t=\log(1+n_t/d_t)$ and its session change $\Delta A_t$ in the regressions. Zero means no retrieved relevant article in the measured interval, not proof that no war news occurred.

\textbf{Sentiment around physical conflict} is represented by negative and uncertainty vocabulary, kept separate from attention. Whole paragraphs containing official/policy subjects or attribution cues are excluded; quoted spans are removed before sentence splitting. Retained sentences require physical-context language and exclude quotation-bearing fragments, personal/institutional pronouns and rhetorical questions. Public-official statements and policy assessments are outside this numerical target. These conservative rules also omit factual reporting and cannot guarantee semantic separation. They narrow the sample without establishing human-validated sentiment labels. Article attention independently retains all relevant original texts.

Using the verified \href{https://sraf.nd.edu/loughranmcdonald-master-dictionary/}{March 2026 Loughran--McDonald dictionary}, category membership requires a positive year flag. Negative flags mean a word was removed. There are 2,345 active negative words and 297 uncertainty words. Alphabetic tokens of at least two characters form the denominator. For category $c$,
\[
L_{ct}=100\frac{\sum_{j\in t}\sum_{w\in c}tf_{wj}}{\sum_{j\in t}N_j}.
\]
Articles with fewer than 20 retained physical-context tokens do not enter lexical aggregation. Pooling counts gives every token equal weight; it is not an equal-article average. No eligible text gives a missing lexical value, not neutral sentiment. The financial dictionary transfers imperfectly to conflict reporting and does not interpret all negation, irony or aspect targets.

The notebook also reports Assignment 1's exact retrospective weighting,
\[
 W_{cj}=\sum_{w\in c:tf_{wj}>0}\frac{1+\log(tf_{wj})}{1+\log(a_j)}\log\frac{N}{df_w},
 \qquad a_j=\frac{N_j}{\text{distinct tokens in }j}.
\]
This is the sum of category weights, not scikit-learn's default normalized TF--IDF. Document frequencies use the eligible full corpus, so this supplement is not a real-time forecasting signal. Literal threat and realized-action patterns provide additional context counts. A diplomacy pattern is retained in the code but is removed by the numerical policy exclusion. These patterns do not turn document tone into the likelihood, severity or duration of war.

\section*{7. Identification through heteroskedasticity}
In the reduced form, market changes respond to an unobserved war factor $w_t$ and other shocks:
\[
\Delta X_t=d\,w_t+u_t.
\]
If loadings are stable, $w_t$ is orthogonal to the other shocks, and \emph{only} its variance changes between news-event sessions $H$ and comparison sessions $L$, then
\[
\Delta\Omega=\Omega_H-\Omega_L=\delta dd',\qquad \delta>0.
\]
This is a positive rank-one covariance restriction. Merely using robust standard errors in OLS is not identification through heteroskedasticity. Rigobon's general simultaneous-equation framework requires informative changes in relative shock variances and sufficient rank; a common scaling of every variance is not enough.

Normalize the two-year Treasury response to one and let $x_t$ be its basis-point change and $y_t$ an outcome. Define $a=\Delta\operatorname{Var}(x)$, $b=\Delta\operatorname{Cov}(x,y)$ and $c=\Delta\operatorname{Var}(y)$. The two moment estimators are
\[
\widehat d_1=b/a,\qquad \widehat d_2=c/b.
\]
The pooled IV estimator instruments $x_t$ with $s_tx_t$ and $s_ty_t$, where $s_t=+1$ in $H$ and $-1$ in $L$. Equal-sized matched regimes make this contrast valid. The centered adaptation demeans separately within each regime and uses the same $1/n$ convention throughout. The original paper assumes zero-mean changes and uses uncentered second moments; that exact convention is also implemented as a sensitivity.

\textbf{Event design.} Twenty-five sourced developments collapse to 24 sessions. Selection uses documented events, not large asset returns. The chronology is retrospective and not preregistered. Nearby controls are selected without replacement, within 35 calendar days and in the same pre-/post-28-February period. Controls cannot be selected events or immediately adjacent trading sessions; their attention is at or below their own month's median. Same-month and then nearest dates are preferred. This does not guarantee less news than each event: two pairs have equal attention and two have greater attention in the control. The labels express a variance hypothesis, not an observed fact.

The 7 April ceasefire announcement at 18:32 EDT maps to 8 April. Weekend events map to the next equity session. Other source dates carry explicit uncertainty flags and are shifted one session in a robustness check. News-body features use the first observed equity session whose 15:00 ET cutoff is at or after the effective timestamp. This is a conservative convention, not exact synchronized observation of every oil, bond and equity close.

The bootstrap resamples matched pairs together, with 1,999 repetitions for the main tables and 399 for sensitivity checks. It preserves within-pair dependence but assumes independence between pairs. When $a\leq0$ or the 95\% bootstrap interval for $a$ includes zero, coefficient intervals are suppressed and the raw estimates remain diagnostic. This is a conservative normalization check, not a formal weak-IV test. No ordinary first-stage statistic is used as proof of identification.

\textbf{Historical comparison.} In the supplied 2003 paper, a war-factor movement normalized to a 25-bp decline in the two-year yield is associated with a 26-bp ten-year-yield decline, a 3.76\% S\&P decline, 5-bp BBB and 34-bp high-yield spread increases, a 0.44\% broad-dollar decline and a \$0.77 rise in 12-month oil futures (pooled estimates, Table 2). Those units and proprietary source series differ from several 2026 proxies. They are reference results, not imposed signs.

\textbf{Variance attribution.} Following the original paper, the incremental component would be $q_j=\widehat d_j^2a$. Its event-session share is $q_j/\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_H(y_j)$; the full-period cumulative-variance calculation is
\[
 \frac{n_Hq_j}{T\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}_{all}(y_j)},
\]
using the actual all-period sample and an additional serial-independence assumption. The notebook supplies these algebraic quantities without clipping negative or excessive shares. Given the weak normalization and poor single-factor fit, \textbf{I do not interpret them as established lower bounds on variance caused by war}. This preserves the paper's calculation while making its failed or uncertain assumptions visible.
\takeaway{Sensitivity to date timing, moments and subperiods rules out a large position based on one attractive coefficient. Monitoring physical supply and reducing unwanted leverage is more defensible than treating a fragile estimated factor as tradable.}

\section*{10. Position, carry and risks}
The proposed 10\% allocation is an analyst risk-budget choice, not an optimized or backtested weight. For an illustrative \$100,000 portfolio, \$10,000 would be allocated to a US Treasury bill maturing in about three months, subject to its actual purchase price and liquidity. The 4.11\% constant-maturity benchmark gives approximate carry of $4.11\%\times0.25=1.0275\%$, about \$102.75 on that sleeve, before costs and taxes.

\section*{13. Limits of the answer}
The strongest observed results concern oil levels, the physical spread, the news-activity pattern and instability of the structural estimates. Remaining limits are the single-outlet news sample, revised body text, selected and sometimes date-only events, imperfect synchronization of daily closes, heterogeneous market proxies, a short sample, potentially changing non-war shocks, incomplete social-platform/media coverage, and the absence of an independently labeled sentiment set. The combination of methods makes these limits testable and visible; it does not remove them.

The original framework is valuable precisely because it does not require directly observing war probability. Its benefit still depends on credible covariance restrictions. Here the model checks limit the conclusion: war-related information belongs in investment monitoring, while a precise causal hedge ratio or a profitable sentiment-timing rule is not established.
